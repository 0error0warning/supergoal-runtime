# The Financial Rationale Behind Pension Reform: Ostonia's 2026 Pension Reform Act in Context

## 1. What Ostonia is doing

The attached Green Paper ("National Pension System Reform — Strategic Overview 2026–2035," Ministry of Social Security, Republic of Ostonia, 12 January 2026) makes two core changes:

- **Retirement age:** phased increase from 60 to 65 over ten years for "Class A" employees, reaching 65 for cohorts born 1976 and later (Table 1 of the PDF; the ministry's online calculator implements the same schedule).
- **Benefit formula:** shift from a *final salary* model to a *lifetime average + inflation adjustment* model, with monthly pension = (average wage over the last 20 years × years of service) / 140, plus a deferral bonus of 4% per year delayed beyond the statutory age.

Both are textbook levers for stabilizing a pay-as-you-go (PAYG) pension system. The sections below explain the general financial logic, the mechanisms by which such reforms restore long-run solvency, and the typical economic consequences, drawing on the supplied research collection.

## 2. The underlying problem: ageing and PAYG arithmetic

Most public pension systems finance current benefits from current payroll contributions — a defined-benefit, pay-as-you-go structure. Fiscal health therefore depends on the ratio of contributing workers to beneficiaries and on how long benefits are drawn (D0039, Tax Foundation). Ageing societies face rising life expectancy alongside declining fertility, pushing the old-age dependency ratio up steadily (D0029, OECD; D0118, McKinsey Global Institute). The OECD notes that defined-benefit PAYG systems are "particularly vulnerable," because contribution rates "struggle to keep up with growing retirement cohorts and longer benefit durations" (D0029). The scale of the pressure is large: McKinsey projects that absent adaptation, GDP-per-capita growth in advanced economies and China could slow by ~0.4% annually from 2023–2050, and retirement systems "might need to channel as much as 50 percent of labor income" to close the financing gap (D0118).

A reform package has only three basic margins to work with: raise contributions (taxes), cut benefits, or raise the retirement age. An i-MIP study of French reforms models exactly these three balancing options — contribution rates, pension levels, or the retirement age — showing the trade-offs are a policy choice about *who* absorbs the adjustment, not whether adjustment is needed (D0004).

## 3. The rationale for each element

### 3.1 Raising the retirement age

**Fiscal logic.** Each added year of work does double duty: it increases contribution inflows and shortens the expected payout period. Austria's reforms raising the early retirement age from 60 to 62 (men) and 55 to 58.25 (women) illustrate the fiscal gain: employment among affected groups rose ~10–11 percentage points, and accounting for higher contributions and income-tax receipts, net government expenditure fell by about €107 million (men) and €122 million (women) per birth-year cohort per year of ERA increase (D0005).

**Growth logic.** Longer careers expand labour supply, the scarcest factor in an ageing economy. In the French modelling, adjusting *solely* through the retirement age (63 → 66.3) produced an annual growth dividend of roughly +0.2 percentage points, driven by higher employment and by stronger saving incentives as individuals face more high-income years (D0004). A rising working-age share directly counters the labour-shortage dynamic that ageing creates (D0125, Investopedia).

**Equity caveat.** Retirement-age increases are regressive in life-expectancy terms: higher-income workers live longer and so collect benefits for more years, while low-income workers in the bottom income quintile have materially shorter remaining life expectancy at 50 (D0001, Brookings; D0003, CRS R44670). Austrian evidence also shows low-wage and less-healthy workers bridge the gap via unemployment or disability benefits rather than working longer — raising spillover costs to UI programs (unemployment rose ~12 percentage points among affected Austrian men) (D0005). Ostonia's Green Paper does not appear to address disability or occupational-exemption carve-outs, a common design weakness.

### 3.2 From "final salary" to "lifetime average + inflation adjustment"

**Fiscal logic.** Final-salary formulas base the pension on the last (typically highest) years of earnings, which tends to over-reward late-career wage growth and generates unequal pension distributions because labour income is most unequal late in the working life (D0113). US public plans illustrate the structure: benefits = years of service × multiplier × final average salary, where the "final" period is often just the last 3–5 or highest years (D0071, Equable). Basing the pension on a 20-year or career average ties benefits to lifetime contributions rather than peak earnings — lowering average outlays, tightening the contribution–benefit link, and reducing windfalls from late promotions.

**Indexation.** Inflation-adjusting the wage history (and the deferral bonus) controls the real growth of liabilities; the choice between wage- and price-indexation is a standard solvency dial.

**Actuarial divisor and deferral bonus.** The Tdivisor = 140 parameter converts accumulated "notional capital" (average wage × service years) into a monthly annuity, and the 4% deferral bonus rewards delayed claiming — an automatic-adjustment-style incentive of the kind the OECD identifies as useful for restoring sustainability (D0113). These are cousins of notional defined contribution (NDC) design: benefits scaled to a divisor that can embed life-expectancy assumptions.

### 3.3 Phased implementation and transition provisions

Phasing by birth cohort (Ostonia's +6 months per two years) spreads the adjustment intergenerationally and gives near-retirees time to adapt — politically and economically important because reforms carry "sizeable political costs" (D0113) and abrupt changes hit workers too close to retirement to respond.

## 4. How such changes ensure long-term financial health

1. **Rebalancing flows:** longer careers raise contribution revenue and shrink the beneficiary-to-worker ratio; lower per-pension outlays reduce the expenditure trajectory (D0029, D0004).
2. **Linking benefits to contributions:** lifetime-average formulas and actuarial divisors make the system respond automatically to changing earnings and lifespans, reducing implicit pension debt (D0113).
3. **Incentives over mandates:** deferral bonuses and actuarial adjustments nudge behaviour rather than forcing it, smoothing labour supply (D0004).
4. **Avoiding the alternatives:** without reform, the burden must be absorbed by taxes or deficits. US Social Security illustrates the alternative path — OASDI costs projected to rise from 4.3% to 6.1% of GDP against revenues of 4.7% (D0106, SSA/NBER), and German generational-accounting work shows declining income-tax revenues compound contribution pressures, shifting costs to future generations (D0060).

## 5. Typical economic consequences

**Positive:**

- **Labour supply and growth:** higher employment of older workers; ~+0.2 pp/year growth potential from retirement-age-led adjustment (D0004); employment gains of 9.75–11 pp in Austria (D0005).
- **Public finances:** lower net expenditure, higher tax and contribution receipts (D0005); reduced pension share of GDP relative to no-reform path (D0106).
- **Macro resilience:** counteracts ageing-driven labour shortages and growth slowdowns (D0118, D0125, D0087).

**Negative / distributional:**

- **Regressive incidence:** lower-income workers have shorter life expectancy, so a uniform retirement age transfers lifetime benefits toward the better-off (D0001, D0003).
- **Spillovers:** higher unemployment-insurance and disability claims among those who cannot work longer; Austrian UI take-up offset part of the savings (D0005).
- **Lower replacement income for some cohorts:** lifetime-average formulas typically cut benefits for workers with steeply rising careers; near-retirees have little time to compensate (D0085 documents France's repeated benefit-side retrenchment under deficit pressure).
- **Aggregate demand risk:** scaling back pensions reduces retiree consumption; NCPERS argues public pension cuts can feed back into lower government revenues via lost spending multipliers (D0010) — a minority, industry-adjacent view, but relevant when reforms are deep.
- **Political cost:** reforms are repeatedly contested (France's cycles; D0085, D0113).

## 6. Limitations and open questions specific to Ostonia

- **No actuarial annex:** the Green Paper gives no projected deficit path, fund balance, or sensitivity analysis, so the claim that the reform "stabilizes" the NPF cannot be verified against the document itself.
- **Coverage unclear:** the schedule applies to "Class A" employees only; the share of the workforce covered, and treatment of others, is unspecified.
- **Missing equity provisions:** no mention of hardship exemptions, disability pathways, or differential treatment for physically demanding occupations — the main documented failure modes of retirement-age hikes (D0005, D0001).
- **Divisor realism:** Tdivisor = 140 (≈11.7 years of payout) is not obviously tied to remaining life expectancy at 65 (typically 15–20+ years); if literal, it is either generous or implicitly relies on inflation adjustment to erode real value — the PDF does not clarify.
- **Country comparability:** evidence cited comes from the US, France, Austria, Germany, and OECD aggregates; Ostonia's demographics, formal-sector share, and capital markets may differ materially, so magnitudes should be treated as indicative, not transferable.
